TT Umpire Study: a flashcard trainer for table tennis umpires

A study app built from the two documents you shared: The Laws of Table Tennis (ITTF 2023-24, issue of 22 July 2024) and the ITTF Handbook for Match Officials, 17th edition (July 2023). App name: “TT Umpire Study”.

In short, I recommend:
  • Spaced repetition + active recall as the engine, with 4 grade buttons (Again / Hard / Good / Easy).
  • About 290 cards across 3 levels (Basic 62 · Intermediate 116 · Advanced 112) and 12 topics. Every card cites its Law or HMO clause.
  • 4 card formats: classic flip, multiple choice, number recall, and “Umpire’s Call” scenarios where you give the decision, the call and the hand signal.
  • A static, offline-capable website (no login). Progress stays in your browser. It can be hosted free on GitHub Pages.
1 · Research

Which learning methods work for rule knowledge

Umpiring needs two things: exact facts (16 cm, 15.25 cm, 13 returns) and quick judgment in a live rally (let or point? who signals? what do I call?). The methods below are among the best-supported findings in learning research. The right column shows how each one shapes the app.

MethodWhat the research showsHow TT Umpire Study uses it
Active recall (testing effect)Retrieving an answer strengthens memory far more than re-reading it.Every card asks first and answers second. There is no “read the rules” mode.
Spaced repetitionReviews spaced at growing intervals beat cramming for long-term retention.An SM-2-style scheduler. Cards you miss come back the same day, and cards you know drift out to weeks.
InterleavingMixing topics forces you to pick the right rule, which is what happens at the table.Review sessions mix topics by default. Topic drills are available but optional.
Elaborative feedbackExplaining why an answer is right helps transfer to new cases.Every answer shows the reason, the source clause and, where relevant, the call and signal.
Scenario-based practicePractising decisions in realistic situations improves judgment in the real situation.“Umpire’s Call” cards describe a rally or incident with a small table diagram.
Confidence gradingRating your own certainty exposes lucky guesses.Each grade sets the next interval. On quiz cards, an “I guessed” toggle downgrades a correct answer.
Mastery progressionLearners do better when they build on solid foundations before moving to complex material.Basic → Intermediate → Advanced. Each level is recommended once about 80% of the previous level is learned.
2 · Structure

Three levels, twelve topics

Basic

Facts every official must know cold: equipment dimensions, definitions, how a game and match are won, what an umpire and assistant umpire are.

Intermediate

Applying the laws during play: service faults, lets and points, serving order, time-outs, towelling, intervals, standard calls and signals.

Advanced

Edge cases and procedure: the expedite system, order errors, penalties and team carry-over, jurisdiction and appeals, score indicators, racket control, Para rules.

Card formats

Flip

Question on the front, answer on the back, then you grade yourself. Best for facts and definitions. About 45% of cards.

Multiple choice

Four options, with distractors built from common mistakes, such as 15.25 cm against 16 cm. About 20%.

Umpire’s Call

A match scenario. You pick the decision. Feedback gives the umpire’s call, the AU’s call and the hand signal. About 25%.

Number recall

Type the number: 2.74, 76, 23, 40, 2.7, 16, 13, 18, 10 minutes. About 10%.

4 · How I would build it

Technical plan

Stack

  • Static site: HTML, CSS and plain JavaScript (or Preact if you prefer components). No backend and no build server needed.
  • Cards in JSON files (data/cards/*.json), kept separate from the app code, so you or other officials can review and edit content without touching code.
  • Progress in localStorage, with Export/Import buttons for backup or moving to another device. Accounts and sync can be added later if you want them.
  • PWA: installable on a phone and usable offline at a tournament venue.
  • Hosting: GitHub Pages from this repository, free with a public URL.

Card data model

{
  "id": "LET-004",
  "level": "intermediate",          // basic | intermediate | advanced
  "topic": "let",
  "format": "scenario",             // flip | mcq | number | scenario
  "prompt": "Score 7-5. A correct service clips the net, then lands on the receiver's court...",
  "diagram": "net-service",         // optional SVG template
  "options": ["Point to server", "Let, repeat 7-5", "Fault, point to receiver", "Let and warn"],
  "answer": 1,
  "explanation": "A service touching the net assembly is a let provided...",
  "umpire": { "signal": "Arm raised above head", "call": "\"Let\" + repeat score" },
  "au":     { "signal": "Arm raised above head", "call": null },
  "refs": [ {"doc": "Laws", "clause": "2.9.1.1"}, {"doc": "HMO", "clause": "11.2.1"} ]
}

Scheduling

SM-2 lite. New card → Again brings it back in 10 minutes, then 1 day, 3 days, 7 days and growing by an ease factor (2.5 default, adjusted by Hard and Easy). A card counts as learned once its interval is 7 days or more. Quiz formats grade automatically: correct = Good, wrong = Again, “I guessed” = Hard. FSRS, a newer and more accurate scheduler, could replace this later without changing any cards.

Modes

  • Review: due cards, interleaved across topics.
  • Learn: introduces up to 10 new cards a day (adjustable), in level order.
  • Drill: one topic or level, without affecting the schedule (optional).
  • Exam simulation: 30–50 mixed questions, timed, with no feedback until the end and a pass mark. The format is to be confirmed with you.

Delivery phases

  1. Content first. I write all ~290 cards as JSON plus a readable review sheet (HTML or CSV) so you can check accuracy before any UI work.
  2. Core app. Home, flip and multiple-choice cards, scheduler, levels, local progress.
  3. Judgment features. Umpire’s Call scenarios with diagrams, number recall, session summary, library and search.
  4. Advanced extras. Doubles serving-order trainer, exam simulation, stats, PWA offline mode, GitHub Pages deploy.

Notes on the sources

  • The uploaded Laws PDF repeats some clause numbers (two 2.1.1, two 2.2.1, two 2.3.1). I plan to cite the official ITTF numbering: for example, net height is 2.2.3 and ball weight is 2.3.2.
  • The HMO notes that WTT events can differ, for example on shirt colours (8.1.4). Cards will follow ITTF rules and flag WTT differences only where the HMO mentions them.
  • The hand-signal figures in Appendix C are images. I would redraw them as simple icons or describe them in words, and I won’t copy the photos.
5 · Your decisions

Questions for you

  1. Level gating: should Intermediate and Advanced be locked until about 80% of the previous level is learned, or open to everyone with a “recommended order” hint?

    My pick: open but recommended. Experienced umpires shouldn’t be blocked.

  2. Exam mode: do you want an exam simulation? If so, how many questions, what time limit and what pass mark? Is it modelled on a specific exam, such as a national umpire exam or the ITTF Advanced Rules exam?
  3. Progress storage: is browser-only progress with export/import enough, or do you need accounts and cross-device sync? Sync needs a backend, such as Supabase.
  4. Tech stack: plain JS static site (simplest to maintain), or React/Preact + Vite?

    My pick: plain JS. It has no build step and is easy for others to edit.

  5. Para content: include the 16 Para cards in the main deck, or keep them as an optional Para pack?
  6. Language: English only, or also another language, such as your national federation’s? Calls stay in English as HMO 18.1.7 requires, but explanations could be translated.
  7. Card count and review: is about 290 cards right, or do you want more depth in some topics, such as service faults and scenarios? Do you want to review the card content (phase 1) before I build the UI?
  8. Look and name: do you like the “TT Umpire Study” name and the table-blue and ball-orange style, or do you have a federation or club brand to match?
